§0 How to read this

This is a decision register, not a roadmap

Each entry below is something someone in the lab wanted to try. For each one you get: what it is, why it is tempting, the honest pros and cons, a proposed verdict, a rough effort estimate, and — most importantly — the line that says what would change our mind.

Framing note — please read

Nothing here is decided. The verdicts are the current thinking of whoever did the legwork, written down so the group has something concrete to attack instead of arguing from vibes. Argue with them. Where an idea is marked rejected you will find the exact number or constraint that killed it — if you can move that number, the verdict moves with it. That is the whole point of writing the numbers down.

Two commitments are not up for revision, because everything else depends on them: extensibility and customization are paramount (every member wants a different creature), and the project does not dictate the final set of emotions, moods or actions — what you find here are starting points, deliberately shaped so the group can replace them without a rewrite.

A-01 do nowXS

“Add to Home Screen” without a service worker

A manifest.json, an apple-mobile-web-app-capable meta tag and one icon, served by the existing esp_http_server. About 40 lines. The buddy's web UI then opens full-screen from the phone home screen with its own icon, over plain HTTP.

Pros

  • Delivers ~80% of what people actually mean by “make it a PWA”
  • Zero TLS, zero heap cost, zero new dependencies
  • Works today on the HTTP server we already ship

Cons

  • Android gives a shortcut, not a full WebAPK install, without HTTPS + SW
  • No offline shell, no push — those need a secure context
  • Icon must be embedded in flash (small, but it is bytes)
Depends onNothing. Can land next session.
What would change our mind If the icon + manifest measurably confuses first-boot provisioning (people tapping the home-screen icon while the buddy is in AP mode and getting a blank page), we gate it behind “connected to your WiFi”.
detail

This is the honest floor of the whole PWA thread. Everything else in A-02 to A-05 is about buying service workers and push on top of it, and each of those purchases costs a lot more than 40 lines. Do this one first regardless of how the rest of the argument goes — it is not a stepping stone, it is a complete feature.

A-02 v2 / laterL

Web Push, delivered by the hub

The device publishes an event; the hub — which has a domain and a real certificate — hosts the PWA, owns the push subscription, and sends the notification. The buddy nudges you when you are not at your desk: “offline for 2 days”, “SD card full”, “someone petted me”.

Pros

  • The only path where the certificate problem simply does not exist
  • Best architectural fit: push is exactly the “device is behind NAT” class of problem the hub was invented for
  • One hub serves N buddies with one origin — see the 3-buddies con opposite
  • Works on iOS, where every other path is fragile

Cons

  • Requires the hub, which does not exist yet
  • Breaks the “device-first, works standalone” promise for this one feature — has to be framed as a hub perk, not a buddy feature
  • Someone has to run and maintain a domain + cert renewal for the lab
Depends onHub server (H-01). Also on someone wanting notifications enough to maintain infrastructure for them.
What would change our mind If a member volunteers a domain and stands up the hub anyway for webhooks/calendar, push becomes nearly free and jumps the queue. Conversely, if nobody can name three notifications they would genuinely want, we drop the whole thread.
detail — how push actually works

Correct the common mental model first: push is not device → phone. The sender makes an outbound HTTPS POST to the browser vendor's push service (FCM, Mozilla autopush, Apple) carrying a VAPID ES256 JWT and a payload encrypted with ECDH-P256 / HKDF / AES128GCM. Every one of those primitives exists in mbedtls, so an ESP32-S3 can send push perfectly well from anywhere with internet.

Sending was never the blocker. Registration is. To get a subscription you need a page in a secure context running a service worker — and that is what the device cannot provide over plain HTTP. See §3 for the full wall.

A-03 open researchM

TLS on the device via local-ip.sh / traefik.me

These services publish a wildcard certificate — and its private key — for hostnames that encode a LAN IP. The device serves https://192-168-1-42.local-ip.sh with a certificate every browser already trusts. Zero infrastructure, works on iOS, unlocks service workers and push registration.

Pros

  • The only zero-infrastructure route to a genuinely trusted origin
  • Satisfies iOS Safari, which refuses everything else
  • No DNS, no ACME client, no renewal robot on the device

Cons

  • The private key is published by design. It satisfies the browser without providing any real confidentiality — anyone on your LAN can decrypt
  • Origin is tied to the IP: a DHCP lease change orphans the installed PWA and its push subscription
  • Depends on a third party's DNS staying up, forever, for free
  • ~30–45 KB heap per TLS session on a device that also wants PSRAM-free internal RAM for audio DMA
Depends onTLS server in the firmware (esp_https_server), static DHCP reservations in practice.
What would change our mind If we decide the web UI never carries anything secret (API keys stay entered over a serial/AP flow), the published-key objection loses most of its force and this becomes a decent hack. Also: measure the heap cost with the audio pipeline running — if TLS and I2S coexist comfortably, this gets much more attractive.
A-04 rejected — cost/benefitXL

Own domain + DNS-01 certificate on the device

The technically correct answer: buy a domain, issue a real Let's Encrypt cert via DNS-01 (the only challenge type that works for a device with no public inbound port), point a record at the buddy's LAN IP.

Pros

  • Actually secure — real confidentiality, private key stays ours
  • Stable origin if paired with split-horizon DNS

Cons

  • A volunteer maker group would be running a DNS-01 renewal robot for a desk toy
  • Every buddy needs its own record; every member needs access to the zone
  • Renewal failure = the buddy's web UI stops loading, which violates “never bricks” in spirit
  • Recurring cost and a single maintainer bus factor
Killed byNot a number — a maintenance model. Correct and horrible.
What would change our mind If the hub happens (H-01), the hub gets the domain and the cert, and this collapses into A-02 for free. That is the intended resolution: the same work, in the right place.
A-05 rejected — browsers refuseS

Self-signed cert, or trusting buddy.local

The obvious first thought: generate a cert on first boot, click through the warning, done. Or: mDNS gives us a nice name, surely buddy.local counts as local enough.

Pros

  • Free, self-contained, no third party
  • mDNS is worth adding anyway for the friendly hostname

Cons

  • Chrome refuses serviceWorker.register() on an origin with a certificate error — even after you click through. Not degraded: zero
  • buddy.local is not a secure context. Only localhost is exempt from the HTTPS requirement
  • A scary browser warning on every first visit, in a workshop, is a support disaster
  • Hosting the PWA elsewhere and pointing it at the buddy fails on mixed-content and Private Network Access rules
Killed byBrowser policy, not effort. There is no amount of work that makes a self-signed origin register a service worker.
What would change our mind A browser policy change (unlikely), or a device-local pairing flow that installs a CA into the phone's trust store (an MDM-grade ask for a desk toy — we are not doing that to our members). mDNS itself is still worth doing for http://buddy.local — just not as a PWA enabler.
B-01 rejected — 106,534 bytes shortM

Run the existing 28.9M-param ESP32 LLM

Someone already did it: slvDev/esp32-ai runs a 28.9M-parameter 4-bit model on an ESP32-S3 with 8 MB PSRAM at 9.88 tok/s. It uses Per-Layer Embeddings borrowed from Google's Gemma 3n, so 25M of the 28.9M parameters live in a memory-mapped flash lookup table and only the needed rows are pulled per token. Genuinely clever.

Pros

  • It exists and it runs — no research risk
  • Proves memory-mapped flash weights are viable on our exact chip
  • The PLE trick is worth stealing even if the model is not

Cons

  • Does not fit. 16,777,216 − 15,623,782 (model) − 65,536 (bootloader + partition table + nvs + phy; the app must start at 0x10000) = 1,087,898 bytes left. Our app binary alone is 1,194,432 bytes. Short by 106,534 bytes before a single byte of pack storage
  • Trained on TinyStories. The author is honest: it writes short coherent stories but cannot answer questions, follow instructions, or recall facts
  • Cannot satisfy the Brain contract — it is a pure continuation model, it cannot emit JSON or pick an emotion. It is an Expression, not a Brain
  • 94.9 ms/token pegs a core, contending with the face render task. The face is the product
Killed byFlash arithmetic. No trim rescues it — see the calculator in §2 and try it yourself.
What would change our mind A 32 MB flash module (H-03) removes the size objection entirely — but the other three objections survive: it still cannot follow the Brain contract, still cannot answer anything, and still costs a core. Size was the least interesting reason to say no.
B-02 open research — lab's chosen directionL

Train a 1–5M param companion-utterance model

Do not chase 28.9M parameters for stories. Train a 1–5M parameter model whose only job is short, in-character one-liners. The lab voted for this and wants a pre-workshop session on it. At this scale the entire budget is vocabulary: TinyStories-1M carries ~3.2M params of embedding table purely because it reuses the GPT-Neo 50,257-token vocab. A custom 1024–2048 token BPE trained on our own corpus fixes that on day one.

Pros

  • Config S is faster than the cloud round-trip (~0.6 s vs ~1.5 s) — this inverts local from “degraded fallback” to “low-latency path”
  • Emotion as a control token emitted first (<happy> oh! that tickles) structurally cannot be malformed — unlike the JSON fences that forced defensive brace-scanning in the Claude path (see main.cpp, brain.reply)
  • Kills repetition, which is the actual enemy of a desk companion — not stupidity
  • The training corpus is a personality definition: a pack could ship its own voice.bin
  • Cheap: ~100k mood-labelled utterances generated with Claude Haiku ≈ $15, plus a couple of GPU-hours on nanoGPT or llama2.c

Cons

  • A trained model has a fixed control-token vocabulary — which is the opposite of extensible, and risks exactly the “dictating the emotion set” outcome the project promised to avoid. See the resolution in §4
  • Never, at any size, does facts, reasoning or instruction-following. It is a renderer, not a thinker
  • Nobody in the lab has trained a language model before — the learning is the point, but it is real time
  • Retraining per-pack is a 20-minute GPU job someone has to be able to run
  • Needs the flash reclamation (C-01) and a raw model partition to land first
Depends onC-01 flash reclamation · a raw (non-LittleFS) partition for esp_partition_mmap · a decision on the two-layer affect vocabulary (§4).
What would change our mind If a trained Config S model turns out to produce output the lab finds embarrassing rather than charming, the whole premise collapses and we stay cloud-only with a much better local chirp table. The pre-workshop session should end with everyone reading 50 sampled utterances and voting. That is the gate.
detail — the capability ladder and why steering is cheap

Steering is cheap at tiny scale; substance is not. A ~3M model will reliably condition on a mood token and produce grammatical, varied, on-tone text. It will never know what day it is. That is fine, because reflexes and sensors already decide the mood — the model's job is to turn a decided mood into words that are never the same twice. Prior art: roneneldan/TinyStories-1M/3M/8M/28M/33M, and crucially the TinyStories-Instruct variants, which prove a 1–3M model can be aimed by a prompt spec.

Full ladder and the interactive sizer are in §4.

C-01 do now — in progressS

Reclaim the 12.44 MB we never mapped

The partition table is a leftover from the classic 4 MB ESP32 proof of concept: it maps 3.56 MB of a 16 MB chip (0x10000 reserved + 0x280000 app + 0x100000 LittleFS), leaving 12.44 MB unallocated and invisible. Being fixed now: a bigger app partition, 4 MB of LittleFS for packs, and a dedicated raw model partition.

Pros

  • Free capacity — the flash is already bought and soldered
  • Unblocks B-02 (weights), content packs, and OTA slots
  • Cheapest high-value change on this entire board

Cons

  • Changing the table means a full erase & reflash — fine now, painful once people have packs on their buddies. Do it before session 1
  • Needs a decision on OTA now, because two app slots double the app cost
Design noteThe model partition must be raw, not a file inside LittleFS: esp_partition_mmap works on raw partitions but not on LittleFS files, and memory-mapping is precisely what makes flash-resident weights viable.
What would change our mind Nothing about doing it. The open question is only the split — how much goes to packs vs. the model partition vs. an OTA slot. Play with §2 and bring a proposal.
D-01 rejected — for the eyesM

Migrate the face renderer to LVGL

LVGL is the default ESP-IDF answer for anything on a screen, and the architecture doc originally assumed it.

Pros

  • Well-trodden path, good ESP-IDF integration
  • Animation timeline and easing for free

Cons

  • The eyes are organic blobs with per-frame geometry (openness, lift, brow slant) — a widget toolkit fights that. Raw framebuffer gives strictly better control
  • An extra abstraction layer between face_model.h and pixels, for a renderer that is already written and working
  • Memory overhead on a chip that also wants audio buffers
Scope of the verdictRejected for the eyes only. See D-02.
What would change our mind If someone demonstrates an LVGL canvas rendering the current parametric eyes at the same frame rate with less code, the argument is over and we switch.
D-02 v2 / laterM

LVGL for on-screen chrome and scrolling text

Menus, setup wizards, scrolling speech text, status screens — everything that is not the creature's face. The current 3×5 bitmap font in face_model.h is charming and will not carry a settings screen.

Pros

  • Text layout, scrolling and fonts are exactly what LVGL is good at
  • Can coexist: LVGL owns a chrome layer, the raw renderer owns the face

Cons

  • Two rendering paths on one 240×240 panel needs a clear ownership rule
  • Nobody has asked for it yet — this is anticipated, not requested
Depends onNothing technically; only on someone wanting an on-device menu.
What would change our mind The first time we need on-device text longer than a few words — likely the moment push-to-talk transcripts appear on screen — this moves to “do now”.
H-01 v2 / laterL

The optional hub server

Already described in the architecture doc as the second implementation of the Brain contract. It is the natural home for everything an ESP32 behind NAT genuinely cannot do: inbound webhooks, OAuth'd email/calendar, long-term memory, cheaper local models — and push notifications.

Pros

  • Contract already designed for it — pointing at a hub URL is a settings change
  • Absorbs A-02, A-04 and long-term memory in one move
  • Any Pi or spare laptop is enough

Cons

  • Someone must run it, forever, or the features that depend on it rot
  • Every feature that lands here weakens the “works standalone” story unless it is explicitly framed as optional
  • Competes for the same volunteer hours as the buddy itself
Rule to keepNothing that lands on the hub may ever become a prerequisite for a buddy being alive and charming. Degrade, never brick.
What would change our mind A member who wants to own it. This is a people question far more than a technical one.
E-01 do now — fun, cheapS

“A fly around the buddy”

A small sprite that wanders the 240×240 canvas while the eyes track it. Drive it with the joystick that is already wired (senses/joystick.cpp, joystick.move → face.look), or let it wander on its own during idle.

Pros

  • Pure charm per line of code — the cheapest personality win on the board
  • Exercises face.look, which already exists
  • Perfect first contribution for a new member: a Reflex, not firmware

Cons

  • Sprite compositing over the eyes needs a draw-order rule in the renderer
  • Risk of becoming a toy nobody turns on — must be pack-authorable, not hardcoded
Depends onA sprite/overlay concept in the face renderer. Small, but it is a real interface decision.
What would change our mind If the overlay layer turns out to cost real frame time on the round face render task, this becomes an idle-only effect rather than a general capability.
B-03 open researchM

Stream model weights from the SD card

If weights could live on the SD card instead of flash, model size stops being a flash-budget problem forever — and every argument in B-01 and H-03 evaporates. This is the highest-leverage unknown on the board.

Pros

  • Unbounded model size, on hardware we already ordered
  • Swapping a personality's voice.bin becomes swapping a file
  • Fits the existing “content packs live on SD” storage tier

Cons

  • SD is a block device with ~1 ms random-read latency; flash is memory-mapped and effectively free to address. Naive per-token embedding-row lookups would very likely collapse throughput
  • Shares the display's SPI bus — contention with the face render path
  • Untested. Nobody has measured whether row caching rescues it
The experimentBenchmark: sequential vs random 4 KB reads on our SD module while the face is rendering, then simulate a token's worth of embedding-row access with and without an LRU row cache. Half a day of work for an answer that reshapes three other entries.
What would change our mind A measurement. Either direction. This is the single most valuable half-day of work on this page — it is the only entry where one experiment can flip multiple verdicts at once.
H-03 needs hardware changeS

Move to a 32 MB flash module

The brute-force answer to every flash argument on this page. Modules exist; the S3 supports it.

Pros

  • Removes the B-01 size objection outright and gives B-02 unlimited headroom
  • Cheap in money terms

Cons

  • The N16R8 devkits are already ordered and delivered (3-pack, per the BOM) — this is a re-order plus a re-verify of the whole bring-up
  • Splits the fleet: packs and docs would have to care which module a member has
  • Solves the least interesting objection to B-01 while leaving all the others
  • B-03, if it works, makes this pointless
Depends onAnswer to B-03 first. Do not buy flash to solve a problem an SD card may already solve.
What would change our mind B-03 failing and a trained Config M/L model proving its worth. Then this is a cheap, boring, correct purchase — and we should make it for the whole fleet at once.
F-01 v1 committedL

Audio: INMP441 + MAX98357A, push-to-talk

Already committed in the architecture doc and the BOM. Listed here because it is the biggest consumer of the same internal RAM, cores and heap that A-03 and B-02 want — it belongs in the same budget conversation as everything else on this board.

Pros

  • PTT deletes the two hardest voice problems: echo/barge-in (half-duplex by construction) and endpointing (release = end of utterance)
  • Parts are ordered; milestone M3 exists for it

Cons

  • DMA and WiFi buffers must live in internal RAM — PSRAM will not do. That is the same pool a TLS server session wants (A-03)
  • 1.5–3 s round-trip latency is irreducible; it can only be masked by a thinking emote
  • Wake word (v2) reopens VAD and barge-in, and Espressif's WakeNet only ships pre-trained words — a custom “Hey Buddy” means microWakeWord or paying Espressif
Interaction with this boardAudio wins any contest for internal RAM. Anything else that wants internal RAM (device-side TLS server) has to prove it coexists.
What would change our mind Nothing about PTT — it is committed. What is open is whether a fast local model (B-02) changes the latency masking design: a 0.6 s local quip while the cloud request is in flight is a much better “thinking” behaviour than a canned chirp.

The renderer, not the thinker

Steering is cheap at tiny scale; substance is not. So the model is a renderer: reflexes and sensors decide the mood, the model turns that mood into words that are never the same twice. The product argument is simple — the enemy of a desk companion is repetition, not stupidity. A buddy that says something dumb but new is alive. A buddy that says the same clever line for the fourth time is furniture.

The tension nobody should paper over

Open conflict — needs a group decision

A trained model has a fixed control-token vocabulary. You cannot add <smug> after training without retraining. That is the exact opposite of extensible, and it is uncomfortably close to the project dictating the emotion set — the one thing the director has said this project must not do.

Proposed resolution — two layers. Argue with it.

layer 1 · affect register (model-side, small, stable)

A deliberately tiny, deliberately abstract set of control tokens the model is trained on. Not emotions — registers. Chosen so they span the tonal space rather than enumerate feelings.

<up> <down> <alert> <calm> <cross> <soft>
layer 2 · expression set (pack-side, unlimited)

Any number of named face expressions a pack invents, each mapping onto a register plus its own eye geometry, colour, blink temperament and sound. No retraining, no firmware change — this is just pack data, exactly like face_model.h becoming pack content in v1.

smug → <up> · betrayed → <down> · caffeinated → <alert> · unimpressed → <cross>

The claim being made is that tone is low-dimensional and identity is not. If that claim is wrong — if the lab tries it and “smug” and “delighted” sound identical because they share a register — then the resolution fails and we need either a larger register set or per-pack retraining as the norm. The convergence that makes the second option less scary: the training corpus already is a personality definition. Fork the corpus, retrain in ~20 minutes, ship voice.bin inside the pack, and you get a genuinely different creature rather than the same creature with a different palette.

Underrated downfalls, even on the paths that “work”

  • Origin identity is tied to the IP. A DHCP lease change orphans the installed PWA and its push subscription. Silently.
  • Three buddies = three separate PWAs. PWAs are origin-scoped. There is no “my buddies” app unless a hub provides the origin.
  • Service-worker cache staleness is a real footgun on a device whose entire selling point is hot-reloading. You would be shipping a caching layer that fights the product's core promise.
  • Offline caching is nearly pointless here. A cached shell still cannot reach the buddy — the buddy is the server. You cache a UI that has nothing to talk to.

Which leaves push, and push alone, as the thing that earns its keep — and only for away-from-desk messages. If the group cannot name three of those it genuinely wants, the honest move is to ship A-01 and close the thread.

§6 How to overturn any of this

Arguing with the register

Every card has a what would change our mind line, and they fall into three kinds:

  • A measurement. B-03 (SD weights), A-03 (TLS heap alongside audio), B-02's throughput. Someone runs a benchmark, the verdict updates. These are the cheapest arguments to win — go get the number.
  • A taste judgement. B-02's output quality, E-01's charm, D-01's rendering. No benchmark settles these; the lab looks at it and votes. Build the smallest thing that lets people look.
  • A people question. H-01, A-02, H-03. These need a volunteer or a budget, not an argument. If nobody wants to own it, that is the answer.

Recommended sequence if you want the most decision-value per hour: C-01 (unblocks everything, half a day) → B-03 benchmark (flips up to three verdicts) → A-01 (40 lines, ship it) → B-02 pre-workshop session, ending with everyone reading 50 sampled utterances and voting on whether the creature is worth building.
